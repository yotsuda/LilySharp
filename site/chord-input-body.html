This page is about chords you play: the notes on the staff, how to write them quickly, and how Lily# checks that a player can hold them. Chord names over the music — chords rows, @chord(Cm7), shapes and diagram layouts — are on Chords and chord diagrams.

Notes in angle brackets

A chord is its notes between < and >, with one duration after the >: <c e g>4. Everything a note takes after its duration — ties, slurs, dynamics, articulations — a chord takes too.

  • Octaves: a chord is read as its first note. The first note takes the octave nearest the note before, as a single note would. Each later note stacks at or above it, so <c e g> and <c g e> are the same chord. The note after the chord is read from that first note. To repeat the bottom note an octave up, mark it: <c e g c'>. A plain c there would be the same C again.
  • Degrees: <1 3 5> is the key's tonic triad and <2 4 6> its ii, spelled from the key signature. A letter and degrees mix: <c 3 5>.
  • Repeats: q repeats the last chord (q' an octave up); a bare duration repeats it at a new length (<f a c>4 4).

The full octave rules, with the marks on later notes that spread a chord out, are in the manual's Notes, rests, chords.

Let completion spell it

In VS Code you rarely type a chord's notes yourself. In music, press Ctrl+Space and the list offers the key's own chords: in C major, C writes <c e g> and Dm7 (also listed as IIm7) writes <d f a c>. For a chord from outside the key, type its symbol in note names and pick it: besm7 offers <bes des f aes>. The notes go in bare, so the octave follows the music around them.

In a violin, viola, cello or double-bass part the same rows write a double stop: the chord's first two notes (Cmaj7 → <c e>), since a bow sounds two strings at once (below).

Lesson 1 of the lessons has you do it on a cello. Every other typing aid is on the editor page.

Name a chord from its notes

A bare @chord on a chord names it from its own notes, so the name can never disagree with what sounds. Sevenths, inversions and slash basses are all named: <e g c> prints C/E.

A name you write yourself — <c' f' g'>1@chord(C) — is checked against the notes, gently (LYS1054, information): F is not a tone of C: the chord's notes spell Csus4, with a quick fix that writes @chord(Csus4). A tension the name leaves out may be meant, so it is never more than a note.

To name a whole song at once, Add Chord Track reads the chords the score plays and writes them as a chords row (from the command line, lysc harmonize suggests a chord track for a melody).

Rolled and broken chords

  • @arpeggio rolls a chord: the wavy line before it. @arpeggio(bracket) draws a bracket instead, to play the notes together.
  • << … >> writes the broken chord out. Its notes play in turn and share the group's duration: << c e g c' >>2 is four eighths. A share that is no plain note value becomes a tuplet by itself. Octaves stack as in a chord. A bare number inside is a degree, never a duration: << c 3 5 8 >>. A member may be a chord or a rest.

On a fretted instrument

A guitar, bass, ukulele or banjo part can hold only what one hand can fret: one note per string, all within four frets. Lily# finds that fingering itself and uses it three ways.

  • A bare @diagram on a chord draws its own notes as fretted: <g b d' f'>1@diagram draws xx5431. It chooses no crossed strings, the lowest position and the fewest fingers. The .ly twin draws the same shape.
  • diagram(NAME SHAPE) goes the other way: you write the shape and it writes the notes. Each string's open pitch plus its fret becomes a note, with its string number, so a tab shows exactly that shape. It draws its diagram too, and a bare @chord adds the name. In << >> its strings are played in turn, lowest first. The diagram is drawn once, over the first note.
  • A chord no hand can hold is warned (LYS1055). The warning names a voicing of the same notes that can be held, written as a diagram writes it.
On a guitarThe compiler says
<e f fis g>1this chord (E3 F3 F#3 G3) cannot be fingered: no string for each note within a hand's reach (4 frets) - a voicing of its notes a player can hold: E3 F#3 G3 F4 (x7406x), the lowest note kept, the others moved by an octave

Ranges, open strings and the tuning are the part's own: instrument ukulele checks against the ukulele's re-entrant G, tuning guitarDropD against the dropped D. Shapes, chord diagram layouts and the capo are on Chords and chord diagrams.

On a bowed string

A bow sounds two strings at once, so a violin, viola, cello or double-bass chord is a double stop. Its two notes must lie on neighbouring strings, within the hand's reach. Anything else is warned (LYS1055):

On a celloThe compiler says
<c g e'>2this chord (C3 G3 E4) cannot be bowed at once: a bowed string sounds two strings together, not 3 - keep two of its notes (a double stop), or break the chord, the low notes first
<c a'>1this chord (C3 A4) cannot be bowed at once: its two notes do not lie on neighbouring strings within the hand's reach (5 semitones) - move one by an octave, or choose another pair of the chord's notes

A three- or four-note chord written as a quick roll is still a fair thing to ask for. Write it as a << >> group, the low notes first, and nothing is warned.

Ask the AI which voicings

Composing in Copilot's chat, the AI can ask Lily# which voicings a player can hold (#chordVoicings). It names a chord symbol, an instrument and a register, and gets back the voicings, commonest first, as Lily# chords with their diagrams: <c g bes ees' g'> x35343 for Cm7 on a guitar. A bowed string's answers are double stops. What the AI then proposes is compiled like anything you write, so a chord no one can hold still shows its LYS1055 warning.

How composing in the chat works is on the editor page.
