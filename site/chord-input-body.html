This page is about chords you play: the notes on the staff, three ways to write them, and how Lily# checks that a player can hold them. What a chord shows over the music — its name, its diagram, a chords row — is on Chord names and diagrams.

WriteWhenSection
<f a c>4you know the notes, or completion writes them1, 2
chord(F)4you know the name; it plays the chord's notes, close3
chord(F 133211)4a guitarist's shape: those strings, those frets3

Notes in angle brackets

A chord is its notes between < and >, with one duration after the >: <c e g>4. Everything a note takes after its duration — ties, slurs, dynamics, articulations — a chord takes too.

  • Octaves: a chord is read as its first note. The first note takes the octave nearest the note before, as a single note would. Each later note stacks at or above it, so <c e g> and <c g e> are the same chord. The note after the chord is read from that first note. To repeat the bottom note an octave up, mark it: <c e g c'>. A plain c there would be the same C again.
  • Degrees: <1 3 5> is the key's tonic triad and <2 4 6> its ii, spelled from the key signature. A letter and degrees mix: <c 3 5>.
  • Repeats: q repeats the last chord (q' an octave up); a bare duration repeats it at a new length (<f a c>4 4).

The full octave rules, with the marks on later notes that spread a chord out, are in the manual's Notes, rests, chords. To print the chord's name over it, add a bare @chord — see Names from the notes.

Let completion spell it

In VS Code you rarely type a chord's notes yourself. In music, press Ctrl+Space and the list offers the key's own chords: in C major, C writes <c e g> and Dm7 (also listed as IIm7) writes <d f a c>. For a chord from outside the key, type its symbol in note names and pick it: besm7 offers <bes des f aes>. The notes go in bare, so the octave follows the music around them.

In a violin, viola, cello or double-bass part the same rows write a double stop: the chord's first two notes (Cmaj7 → <c e>), since a bow sounds two strings at once (below).

Lesson 1 of the lessons has you do it on a cello. Every other typing aid is on the editor page.

By name or by shape

Instead of its notes, write the chord's name, or a guitarist's shape (one character per string, low string first: x32010 is C). One rule covers every spelling: the word says what is seen — chord shows the name, diagram the fret diagram — and with no @ the chord sounds.

WrittenPrintsSounds
chord(F)4FF A C, close
chord(F 133211)4F over its diagramthat shape's strings
chord(x32010)4C, read off the shape, over its diagramthat shape's strings
diagram(133211)4the diagram alonethat shape's strings

The same words with @ — c1@chord(F), c1@diagram(133211) — only mark the note they are on; they are on Chord names and diagrams.

  • The notes: each string's open pitch plus its fret, on the part's instrument (else the guitar), each with its string number, so a tab shows exactly the shape. chord(C x32013)1 on a guitar is C3 E3 G3 C4 G4. They are absolute — octave marks after the ) are an error; play it higher on the neck instead — and the note after the item is read from its lowest note.
  • No shape: chord(G) plays the chord's notes close, G B D, its root by middle C as the staff draws it (by a bass staff's foot on a bass staff), in the instrument's range — on any part, a guitar's too — and draws no diagram. diagram(G) plays and draws its usual shape: the instrument's, else the guitar's. A shape written badly, or only for another instrument, warns (LYS1040) and keeps its time as a spacer.
  • The tail is a chord's: a duration, dots, ties, slurs, beams, articulations, dynamics; a bare duration or q repeats it.
  • Strum after strum: write every strum the same. The name and the diagram stand where the chord changes, and again at the head of each system, and the repeats take no room.

In the editor

  • Ctrl+Shift+Up / Down on an item walks the other shapes of the same chord, and sounds each one; from chord(G), Down writes the usual shape.
  • Found a shape by ear? Write chord(x02210)4 and the page prints its name, Am. Or hover diagram(x02210): it says this shape is Am, and the quick fix (Ctrl+.) writes the name in.
  • Right-click an item → Convert to Chord Notes <…> writes it out as the notes it plays, octave marks and string numbers included — diagram(133211)4 becomes <f,\6 c\5 f'\4 a'\3 c'\2 f''\1>4, and a chord(…) item keeps its name as a bare @chord (chord(F)1 → <f a c>1@chord). The file must sound the same afterwards, or nothing changes.

Rolled and broken chords

  • @arpeggio rolls a chord: the wavy line before it. @arpeggio(bracket) draws a bracket instead, to play the notes together.
  • << … >> writes the broken chord out. Its notes play in turn and share the group's duration: << c e g c' >>2 is four eighths. A share that is no plain note value becomes a tuplet by itself. Octaves stack as in a chord. A bare number inside is a degree, never a duration: << c 3 5 8 >>. A member may be a chord or a rest.
  • A shape inside is spread: << diagram(C x32010) >>2 plays the five strings in turn, lowest first, under 5:4, and draws the diagram once, over the first note. Mixed with other members (<< c diagram(G 320003) e >>) its notes take their places in the sequence.

On a fretted instrument

A guitar, bass, ukulele or banjo part can hold only what one hand can fret: one note per string, all within four frets. Lily# finds that fingering itself — no crossed strings, the lowest position, the fewest fingers — and uses it four ways.

  • A bare @diagram on a chord draws its own notes as fretted: <g b d' f'>1@diagram draws xx5431, and follows the notes when you edit them. The .ly twin draws the same shape. On a single note it draws where that note is held: c'1@diagram is xxxx1x, and Ctrl+Shift+Up walks the other places (xxx5xx, xx-10-xxx, …).
  • Right-click a <…> chord → Add Guitar Diagram writes that shape out: <g b d' f'>1@diagram(xx5431). With the caret on the shape, Ctrl+Shift+Up / Down walks the other ways to hold the same notes (fingering 2 of 3: x-10-976x); the notes never change. On a bare @diagram, which already draws the first, Down writes that one out; Up at the first takes the shape away again, and the diagram follows the notes once more. On a single note of a guitar part the light bulb (Ctrl+.) offers it too.
  • Right-click a <…> chord → Convert to Chord Symbol chord(…) makes it an item again: <f,\6 c\5 f'\4 a'\3 c'\2 f''\1>1@chord(F) → chord(F 133211)1. The name is its @chord(NAME), else read off its notes; chord(NAME) alone when its close voicing is the notes, else with the shape. The file must sound the same, or nothing changes.
  • A written diagram that no longer sounds its chord is warned (LYS1057) — the notes were edited after the shape was written. The quick fix writes the chord's own shape. A capo or a transposing part, every note moved alike, is not a difference.
  • A chord no hand can hold is warned (LYS1055). The warning names a voicing of the same notes that can be held, written as a diagram writes it.
On a guitarThe compiler says
<e f fis g>1this chord (E3 F3 F#3 G3) cannot be fingered: no string for each note within a hand's reach (4 frets) - a voicing of its notes a player can hold: E3 F#3 G3 F4 (x7406x), the lowest note kept, the others moved by an octave

Ranges, open strings and the tuning are the part's own: instrument ukulele checks against the ukulele's re-entrant G, tuning guitarDropD against the dropped D. Which instrument a diagram is drawn for, every-chord diagrams and the capo are on Chord names and diagrams.

On a bowed string

A bow sounds two strings at once, so a violin, viola, cello or double-bass chord is a double stop. Its two notes must lie on neighbouring strings, within the hand's reach. Anything else is warned (LYS1055):

On a celloThe compiler says
<c g e'>2this chord (C3 G3 E4) cannot be bowed at once: a bowed string sounds two strings together, not 3 - keep two of its notes (a double stop), or break the chord, the low notes first
<c a'>1this chord (C3 A4) cannot be bowed at once: its two notes do not lie on neighbouring strings within the hand's reach (5 semitones) - move one by an octave, or choose another pair of the chord's notes

A three- or four-note chord written as a quick roll is still a fair thing to ask for. Write it as a << >> group, the low notes first, and nothing is warned.

Ask the AI which voicings

Composing in Copilot's chat, the AI can ask Lily# which voicings a player can hold (#chordVoicings). It names a chord symbol, an instrument and a register, and gets back the voicings, commonest first, as Lily# chords with their diagrams: <c g bes ees' g'> x35343 for Cm7 on a guitar. A bowed string's answers are double stops. What the AI then proposes is compiled like anything you write, so a chord no one can hold still shows its LYS1055 warning.

How composing in the chat works is on the editor page.
