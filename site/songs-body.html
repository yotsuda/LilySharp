How these were made

Every piece on this page was composed by an AI model, asked in plain words. A person asked, listened, and asked again; the notes and the words are the model's. Each one is a single .lys file — text — and the file is under its video, whole, to read or to open in Lily# Studio and change.

The model writes text, and Lily# checks it

A score in Lily# is plain text, so a language model can write one from its first bar to its last. Lily# then tells it what it wrote: lysc check finds a bar that is too long or a word with no note, and lysc review reads the piece as music — a part that keeps one figure through every section, chords too low to sound clear, a tune that is all eighth notes — and says what to look at. The model answers those and writes again.

For a guitar it asks Lily# which shapes a hand can hold; the off-beat chords of the Zundamon song below were moved up the neck that way.

The words are sung, the page is filmed

The songs are sung by voices on the composer's own machine, through Cantoria: a singer line in the file names the voice. Nothing was recorded. Each video is the preview's Export as MP4 — the engraved page, lit note by note as it plays.

A video is one output among many. The same file goes out as a PDF, SVG or PNG of the page, as MIDI, WAV or MP3, and as MusicXML, LilyPond or a VOCALOID sequence for other programs — every one a command.

One of the songs is in four languages: the same tune, with words written for each, as four scores of one file.

The header of each file says which model wrote it and what it was asked. Most were written by Claude in Claude Code; the Zundamon song was written in GitHub Copilot's chat, with the tools Lily# Studio gives a chat.

Zundamon and Metan Introduce Themselves

ずんだもんとめたんの自己紹介 — a bright pop song in B-flat major, in Japanese, for two voices and a band. Zundamon and Metan introduce themselves in turn, two bars each, and sing the chorus together; the last chorus repeats its last line.

This one was composed in GitHub Copilot's chat. Its guitar first played full chords low on the neck, under the bass; asked to move them up and thin them out, the model took three-note shapes on strings 2 to 4 from Lily#'s chord voicings.

Spring Breeze Bicycle

An upbeat pop song in F major for a voice, electric piano, bass and drums. The verse stays low over a pushed bass; the chorus opens high on “run, run” over four on the floor and climbs to F5 in its last line; the bridge drops to half time before the last chorus.

It was written in Korean. The English, Japanese and Chinese words were then written to the same notes — as many syllables, morae or characters as each bar has notes — and each language is a score of its own in the one file, with its own title and its own singer.

One file, as many scores as you ask for

The music is written once: the parts, the sections, the form. A score { } block only says what to print of it — which staves, in which order, with which words under them — so another layout of the same music is a few more lines at the end of the file, and no note is copied. This file ends with five of them. Two are:

score english {
  title "Spring Breeze Bicycle"
  singer "diffsinger/Nishiren" on vox sings en
  staff vox
  lyrics en
  staff keys
  staff bass
  staff kit
}

score english_vocal {
  title "Spring Breeze Bicycle"
  singer "diffsinger/Nishiren" on vox sings en
  staff vox
  lyrics en
}

The first is the full score in English; the second is the singer's part alone — the same bars, the same words, three staff lines fewer. The Korean, Japanese and Chinese scores differ from the first in the title, the singer and one word, the name of the lyrics. A conductor's score and every player's part, a lead sheet and a tablature, a page for each language: all are scores of one file, and a change to a note shows in every one of them.

Each score is written out under its own name — Spring-Breeze-Bicycle-english_vocal.pdf beside Spring-Breeze-Bicycle-korean.pdf — and the preview shows the one you pick. The video of the singer's part below is that score's page, with the whole band in its sound. More on scores.

Paper Boats

A piano ballad in D major, in English. The verse comes in off the beat and stays low; the chorus opens on its highest note with the title words, and its last line is sung once more, broad, at the end.

Moon on the River

江上月 — a ballad in G major for a baritone, flute and piano, in Chinese, its tune on the five notes G A B D E. The verse is low and even; the chorus opens on its highest note calling the moon, and closes on the tonic only the second time round.

A Window After the Rain

A piano piece in G major. Its two-bar theme falls a sixth and settles through an appoggiatura; the middle section turns it upside down in E minor, the return climbs to B5, and a deceptive cadence leads to a quiet close.

A note to the player, written in Markdown

The boxed note under the title, on the first page of the video above, is part of the file. A page's text is written as Markdown between two fence lines — bold, italic, headings and lists as anywhere else — and the opening line says where it goes and how: start or end of the score, box for a frame, xMargin to narrow it. The model wrote this one for whoever plays the piece:

```md start box xMargin 20mm
**To the player.** Let the left hand ring: one pedal to a bar, changed on the bass note.

- The theme falls and then *leans* on its long note (bars 2 and 4). Arrive a little late and let it fade.
- In the middle section the same shape climbs instead of falling. Play it **softer** than the opening, not louder.
- The octaves at the return are the one *forte* of the piece. Everything after them is an afterthought.
```

Evening Calm Bossa

A bossa nova in F major for flute, piano, bass and drums. The flute's theme comes in off the beat and is held over the bar line; the B of its AABA passes through a backdoor ii–V and climbs to D6. The piano plays the batida in rootless chords, the bass roots and fifths.

The harmony, bar by bar

A language model does not hum a tune and then look for chords. It chooses the harmony, and writes the melody and the accompaniment out of it. This piece shows that plainly, so here is what it chose and why — every statement below can be checked against the notes in the file.

One thing first: the piano never plays a root. The bass has it. Each chord is the piano's three notes over the bass note, the way a pianist plays in a band.

The three chords everything starts from

From Gm7 to C9 only one note moves: F falls a half step to E, the seventh of one chord becoming the third of the next, while B♭ and D stay where they are. That half step is the sound of a ii–V, and it is why the two chords are heard as one gesture.

A — the same eight bars, with two endings

  • Bars 1–4: the theme is made of the chords. The flute's C E A over Fmaj7 are its fifth, seventh and third. Bars 3–4 repeat the shape a step lower because the harmony has moved a step, from I to ii — a sequence, not a new idea.
  • Bar 6, first time: a chord from outside the key. F major would give Dm7. D7♭9 has two notes the key does not: F♯ and E♭. They close in on G and D from either side — and G and D are the root and fifth of the Gm7 that follows. The flute plays exactly those two notes, F♯ then E♭, so the melody itself tells the ear that the chord has changed.
  • Bar 8, first time: left open. The phrase stops on C9, the dominant, so the tune has to come round again.
  • Second time: the plain chord, and home. Bar 6 is now Dm7, and Am7 → Dm7 → Gm7 → C9 → F6 falls by fifths all the way to the tonic. The first ending asks a question with a borrowed chord; the second answers it with the chords of the key alone.

B — three ways of leaving F, and the way back

Bar12345678
ChordB♭maj7B♭maj7B♭m7 E♭9Fmaj7Em7♭5 A7Dm7G7Gm7 C9
IVIVbackdoor ii–VIii–V of viviV of Vii V
  • Bars 1–2: the subdominant. The first long chord that is not F. The drums move to the ride cymbal and the piano holds its chords, so the change of harmony is also a change of light.
  • Bars 3–4: the backdoor. B♭m7 → E♭9 is a ii–V that belongs to A♭ major, but it lands on F. It works because E♭9's D♭ and G slide by a half and a whole step into the C and A of Fmaj7. The flute's A♭ here is the first note of the tune taken from F minor.
  • Bars 5–6: the same shape, aimed elsewhere. Em7♭5 → A7 → Dm7 is a ii–V into the relative minor; A7's C♯ leads up to D.
  • Bar 7: the brightest chord. G7, the dominant of the dominant, has a B♮ where the key has B♭. The piece's one forte is here, and the flute climbs to its highest note outlining G B D — the chord, again, in the melody.
  • Bar 8: the way back. B♮ falls to B♭: G7 becomes Gm7, the bright chord turns into the ordinary ii, and the bass walks C D E up to the F that begins A again.

Coda

Fmaj7 → B♭m6 → Fmaj7 → Fmaj9. B♭m6 is IV made minor: its D♭ falls to C as it resolves — a last, quieter echo of the backdoor in B, and the flute sings the chord's own notes, D♭ F B♭. The final chord adds the ninth, G, and the flute ends on A, the third: the piece closes at rest, but not shut.

North Wind Waltz

A waltz in D minor for string quartet. The second violin murmurs like wind under a theme that rises and settles through an appoggiatura; each phrase ends in a hemiola. The cello sings the middle section in F major. Then the theme's head is tossed from part to part over a pulsing dominant, the return comes in octaves and climbs to D6 by way of a Neapolitan chord, and after the cello's last word a gust closes it.

Its first version was found a little dull by its listener — one accompaniment figure from start to end, every phrase in the same four bars — and the model rewrote it: this is the second.
