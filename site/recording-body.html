What it does

Give it a recording of a song, and it writes the chord chart. Right-click an .mp3 or a .wav, choose Convert Recording to Lily# (.lys)…, and about half a minute later a chart is open: the tempo, the key, the bar lines, the sections and the chord of every bar.

Then it does what a chart on paper cannot. Press ▶ and the recording plays under the chart, the bar that sounds lit on the page. Click a bar and the record starts there. Loop four bars, slow them down without changing their pitch, and turn the band's parts on and off — the bass alone, or everything but the singing.

If you play in a band, you know both halves of this job: working out the chords of a song from the record, and playing along with it until you have it. Here they are one tool, and what you end up with is not a screen in an app but a chart you own — text you can correct, transpose, print and hand to the rest of the band.

Windows only, and a preview: the reading is a first draft to correct, not a finished chart. Everything runs on your own computer; no recording is sent anywhere.

Three steps

  1. ConvertIn VS Code's Explorer, right-click the recording and choose Convert Recording to Lily# (.lys)… (Preview). The chart is written beside it and opens with its preview.
  2. Listen and correctPlay the chart with the record. Where a chord is wrong, type the right one — or, where the reader itself was in doubt, try its second guess with one key.
  3. Practise, print, shareLoop the hard bars with your own part turned off. Transpose for the singer. Print a PDF for rehearsal, or export a video of the chart with the record in it.

To show a reading that anyone may check, this one was made from a piece whose answer is known: Evening Calm Bossa, the bossa nova on the songs page. Its score was played into a WAV file, a minute and a half long, and that file — only the sound — was given to the reader. Eighteen seconds later it had written this:

So: the frame of the song — tempo, key, bars, form — is right, most chords have the right root and kind, the ninths and other colours are left for you to add, and a few bars are wrong. That is what to expect, and a recording of a real band is harder than this one was (the numbers are below).

The chords it doubts are marked, with its second guess

A reader that is sometimes wrong is only useful if it says where it may be wrong. Where it could not decide between two chords it writes both:

Am7 | D7@or(Dm7) | Gm7 | C@or(Csus4) |

The chart shows and plays the first, D7; the other waits in the @or.

  • One key tries the other. With the caret on the chord, Ctrl+Shift+Up / Down swaps the two and plays the chord.
  • Hear it in the music. Ctrl+Shift+PageUp / PageDown swaps them and plays the whole bar — the recording's bar and the chart's chord together, so your ear decides.
  • Any chord can be typed. The chart is text: write D7-9 over D7 and it is so. How chords are written →

The band, one part at a time

While it reads the chords, the converter also takes the recording apart. Six parts are written beside the chart — vocals, guitar, piano, other, bass and drums — and each gets a line in the preview's practice panel, with a switch and a fader.

  • Hear one part alone. Turn the others off, or press its S: the bass line you are trying to work out, with nothing over it.
  • Take one part out. Turn off the part you play, and the rest of the band is your backing track.
  • Bass players: an octave up. Low notes are hard to tell apart. Octave raises the recording an octave, at the same speed, and the line is suddenly easy to hear.
  • Nothing is lost when all are on. With every part on and every fader at 100% you hear the original recording itself, not the parts added up again.

The separation is done by a published model, on your machine. It is good enough to hear a line and to practise against; it is not a clean studio stem.

Practise with the record

Because the chart knows when each bar begins in the recording, everything the preview does with a score it does with the record too.

You want toDo this
start from a placeclick the bar; Play starts there
repeat a passageselect its bars and turn Repeat on — with a bar of count-in before each round, if you like
slow it downthe Tempo slider, down to half speed; the pitch stays where it was
play it in another keywrite transpose in the chart: the chords change, and the recording is shifted to match
hear one bar againAlt+M plays the bar the caret is in, with the recording
hear the chart against the recordturn both on: the chart's chords, or any part you add to it, sound with the recording, bar for bar

The chart follows the band, not a metronome: each bar of the chart is stretched or squeezed to the bar of the recording, so a drummer who pushes the chorus stays in step with the page. More on practice playback →

A chart that is yours

What the converter writes is an ordinary Lily# file. It has no staves yet, only the chords, bar by bar, under their section names — and everything Lily# does is open to it.

For rehearsal

Print it as a PDF. Rename the sections Verse and Chorus. Add guitar or ukulele diagrams over the names, or a capo. Chords and chord diagrams →

To build on

Add the melody, the words, a bass line or the kicks the band must hit — by hand, or by asking AI. The chart becomes a lead sheet, then a score, from the same file.

To share

Export as MP4 writes the chart with the recording in it, each chord name lit while it sounds. WAV and MP3 take the recording too, with the parts as you set them.

A chart you already have

Attach Recording… ties a recording to a chart you wrote yourself, so it plays with yours. And if you convert a song you have already charted, the new reading can be written beside yours, to compare.

The recording itself is never copied or changed, and nothing about it is written into the chart: a small file beside the chart remembers where the recording is and when each bar begins.

What to expect

Measured on 100 studio recordings of pop songs with a band and singing (the RWC Popular Music Database):

It gets rightHow often
the bar lines94%
the chord's root (the C of Cm7)87% of the time
the root, and major or minor82% of the time
  • It is a first draft. Roughly one chord in six has the wrong root or the wrong major or minor. The finer colours — sevenths, ninths, altered notes — need your ear more often still.
  • Not yet handled: songs whose tempo changes a great deal, and bars with no chord at the start of a song.
  • It reads harmony, not notes. It does not write the melody, the bass line or the drum part. Those you add.

What you need

  1. Windows, 64-bit, and Lily# Studio.
  2. The Orecchiante extension, which holds the reader and its models. Lily# Studio has the menu; the first time you choose Convert Recording to Lily# (.lys)… without it, you are offered the install. Orecchiante is free and open source (its repository), and a separate program, as Cantoria is for singing.
  3. A recording of five seconds or more that Windows can read: WAV, MP3, M4A, MP4, AAC, FLAC or WMA.

A three-minute song takes about half a minute. The chart, the small file of bar times and a folder of the six parts are written to the folder you choose; the parts of a five-minute song take a few tens of megabytes.

The music is someone's. Working out a song for your own practice is one thing; publishing a chart of it, or a video with the recording in it, is another, and needs the rights holder's leave. The piece in the example above was written for this site.
